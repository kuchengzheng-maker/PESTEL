Brief: How have (a) the ban on Chinese EVs and (b) the expiration of the federal EV tax credit affected the pricing and reach of electric vehicles for American consumers? For the Chinese-EV ban, benchmark U.S. prices against Mexico, Canada, and Europe.

Baseline: September 2026

1.0The core finding

Two political decisions — one that removed the cheapest possible supply, and one that removed the largest demand-side subsidy — have moved in the same direction at the same time. Together they have done something unusual: they raised the floor price of an electric car in America while simultaneously lowering the ceiling on how many Americans can afford one.

The result is a market that has stopped being a mass market and reverted to a discretionary one. U.S. EV share peaked at 7.5% of new-vehicle sales in Q3 2025, collapsed to 5.8% in Q4 2025, and was still at roughly 5.8–6% through the first half of 2026. Meanwhile the cheapest new EV an American can buy costs about $29,000, against roughly $18,000 in Canada, $22,700 in Mexico, and $23,200 in Europe.

One qualification belongs up front, because it changes how the price gap should be described. Adjusted for local incomes and local vehicle price levels (§1.3, Comparison 7), the American EV market is not unusually expensive — proportionally it sits level with Germany and well below Mexico. The U.S. anomaly is not the price level; it is the price floor. The American EV average is high because the cheap models were removed from the market, not because any individual car is overpriced. That is a claim about distribution, not level — and it is the more defensible one.

Two control comparisons pin the cause down. First, an identical non-Chinese vehicle — the Tesla Model Y Standard — costs within about 10% of the same amount in all four markets on a tax-adjusted basis, which rules out currency, cost, tax, and regulatory explanations for the entry-level gap. Second, the United States is the only one of these four markets where EV adoption went backwards in 2026: Europe +35%, Canada forecast +40%, Mexico +44%, the U.S. −28%.

Neither policy is an accident or a side-effect. Both are the intended operation of deliberate industrial policy. The consumer-facing cost of that policy is the subject of this section.

1.1The "ban" on Chinese EVs is not one policy — it is three walls

It is analytically important to separate the instruments, because they behave differently and they fail differently. Only one of them is an actual ban.

Instrument type Price instrument — raises cost, can be engineered around Prohibition — bars sale outright, no price clears it

Wall 1Section 301 tariff: 100% on Chinese EVs

The Section 301 tariff on Chinese-built battery-electric vehicles was raised from 25% to 100%, effective 27 September 2024, and has been maintained through 2026. Stacked with the 2.5% MFN passenger-car duty, a Chinese-built EV faces roughly 102.5% before any other measure.

This is a price instrument. It does not prohibit the vehicle; it prices it out.

Wall 2Section 232 automotive tariff: 25% on imported vehicles

Separately, the Section 232 national-security tariff imposed 25% on imported passenger vehicles from 3 April 2025 and on parts from 3 May 2025, with partial carve-outs for USMCA-compliant goods. This is not China-specific — it hits German, Japanese, Korean, and UK imports too — but it stacks on top of Walls 1 and 3 and it is a major reason every EV in America got more expensive, not just Chinese ones.

Its scale is significant: average new-vehicle prices in Q1 2026 ran $8,000–12,000 above Q4 2024 levels, of which roughly $4,000–6,000 is attributed to the tariff component. Imported models absorbed $5,000–8,900 per vehicle; even domestically-assembled models rose $1,600–2,000 on steel, aluminum, and parts costs.

Wall 3The connected-vehicle rule: the actual ban

This is the decisive instrument, and it is the one most commentary under-weights.

On 16 January 2025 the Commerce Department's Bureau of Industry and Security finalized Securing the ICTS Supply Chain: Connected Vehicles, effective 17 March 2025. It prohibits the import or sale of connected vehicles containing covered software or vehicle-connectivity hardware with a Chinese or Russian nexus:

  • Software prohibitions bite from model year 2027 — i.e. vehicles on sale now.
  • Hardware prohibitions bite from model year 2030 (or 1 January 2029 for hardware not tied to a model year).
  • Critically, a manufacturer owned by, controlled by, or subject to the jurisdiction of China is barred from selling a completed connected vehicle in the U.S. from MY2027 regardless of where the software or hardware came from, and regardless of where the car was assembled.

That last clause is the whole ballgame. It converts a tariff problem into a market-access prohibition. BYD cannot tariff-engineer its way in by assembling in Mexico, Hungary, or Brazil, because the prohibition attaches to the manufacturer's ownership, not the vehicle's origin. There is no price at which the vehicle becomes legal.

A pending bill, the Connected Vehicle Security Act of 2026 (Sens. Moreno and Slotkin), would codify and broaden this into a statutory ban on Chinese-linked vehicles and components — hardening what is currently a regulation into law, and insulating it from a future administration's rulemaking.

Why the distinction matters for consumers

If only Walls 1 and 2 existed, the U.S. would have expensive Chinese EVs. With Wall 3, it has none, at any price, for the foreseeable future. The policy question is therefore not "how much do tariffs add to the sticker?" It is "what does it cost the American consumer to have an entire price segment removed from the market?"

Reading the table honestly — three caveats

Caveat 1 — tax basis. European prices are quoted VAT-inclusive; U.S. prices are quoted before sales tax. Stripping German VAT puts the Dolphin Surf at roughly $22,500 on the same basis as a U.S. sticker. That widens the real gap rather than narrowing it.

Caveat 2 — segment. The Dolphin Mini / Dolphin Surf / Seagull is an A-segment city car (~3.8 m, 30 kWh, 220–300 km). The Bolt is a B-segment hatch with 262 miles EPA range and a 65 kWh pack. These are not the same product, and a straight price line under-states the Bolt's content.

But that caveat is itself the finding. The United States has no A-segment EV at all. Not an expensive one — none. The comparison isn't "American A-segment cars cost more"; it's that the segment does not exist here, because policy removed the only firms currently willing to build in it at volume. Mexico, Canada, and Europe all have a ~$18,000–23,000 electric car on sale. America's price floor starts around $29,000, and that floor is set by regulation, not by engineering.

Caveat 3 — Canada's number is a launch commitment, not yet a transacted price. BYD confirmed Canadian entry on 8 June 2026 — 20+ dealerships in Toronto, Vancouver, Montreal, and Calgary, four models (Seagull, Dolphin, Atto 3, Seal), from C$25,000 to C$44,990. Only the C$25,000 entry price is company-confirmed; the rest are dealer guidance and overseas comparables. Treat the Canadian figure as a forward indicator.

What the three regimes actually demonstrate

  • Mexico proves tariffs are a price instrument, not a wall. A 50% duty cut Chinese car imports 45.3% year-on-year in January 2026 (to US$188m) — a real effect. But BYD still lists the Dolphin Mini at MXN 399,800 and still sells it. Mexican consumers pay more than they would in a free market; they are not denied the product. The Mexican government forecasts MXN 52bn (~US$2.8bn) in 2026 revenue from the measure.
  • Canada proves the exclusion was a political choice, not a technical necessity. Ottawa adopted a 100% surtax in 2024 explicitly to align with Washington, collected roughly $2m in total revenue from it (because it blocked everything), and repealed it in March 2026 for a 6.1% in-quota duty forecast to raise over $100m a year. Canada concluded that a calibrated duty beat a prohibition on both revenue and consumer grounds. The same continent, the same security literature, the opposite answer.
  • Europe proves a middle path is administrable. Firm-specific countervailing duties (17.0%–35.3%) scaled to each manufacturer's subsidy level, now supplemented by a minimum-price undertaking mechanism, have protected European producers while leaving a €19,990 electric car on the market. BYD became Germany's best-selling PHEV brand in May 2026 — evidence that the EU's approach constrains rather than excludes.

The bottom line on the ban

For the American consumer, the ban's price effect is not a markup that can be measured on any window sticker. It is an absence. The measurable consequence is that the U.S. entry price for an electric car sits roughly $6,000–11,000 above that of every comparable market — and unlike a tariff, that gap cannot be competed away, because the competitor is legally barred rather than merely taxed.

One further calibration, from Appendix A: the rung missing from the American ladder is a $20,000–23,000 car, not a $10,000 one. The same BYD Seagull that lists at roughly $9,200 ex-tax in China sells for about $18,050 in Canada, $19,533 in Germany, and $19,583 in Mexico — every export market roughly doubles the Chinese price, including the ones where the car is entirely legal. The relevant counterfactual for the U.S. is therefore Canada, Mexico, and Europe, never China's domestic price.

State that carefully, though. Comparison 7 in §1.3 shows this nominal gap narrows to roughly 13% against Canada, and to nothing against Germany, once local incomes and vehicle price levels are taken into account. The durable finding is therefore not that Americans pay more per car — proportionally they do not — but that the American market has no entry rung at all, which is a fact about what is available rather than about what things cost.

The reach effect follows arithmetically. An $18,000–23,000 EV is a plausible second household car, a first car for a young buyer, and a viable purchase for the ~40% of U.S. households whose new-vehicle budget tops out near $30,000. A $29,000-and-up EV market is a market for people who were probably buying a new car anyway.

1.3Seven further comparisons

The A-segment comparison in §1.2 is the headline, but it rests on a single non-identical vehicle class. The following seven comparisons attack the same question from independent angles — three cross-border, three internal to the U.S. market, and a seventh that tests the whole set against income and local price levels. Six converge; the seventh materially qualifies them, and is reported in full for that reason.

1The same-model control: Tesla Model Y Standard in four markets

If American EVs were expensive because of general cost, currency, labour, or market-structure differences, then every EV would be more expensive here, not just the cheap ones. The cleanest way to test that is to price an identical, non-Chinese vehicle across all four markets.

The Model Y Standard is the ideal control: same manufacturer, near-identical specification, sold in all four markets, and completely unaffected by Chinese-EV policy.

This is the single most important comparison in the section, because of what it rules out. The U.S. entry-level premium documented in §1.2 is not an artifact of exchange rates, tax accounting, labour costs, dealer margins, safety regulation, or American consumer preference for larger vehicles. Those forces would show up in the Model Y row, and they don't.

The gap exists at the bottom of the market and only at the bottom of the market — which is precisely where the excluded competitor would have sat.

That emptiness is the mechanism by which the policy reaches consumers. A household with a $25,000 ceiling is not offered a worse EV in America — it is offered none.

Two findings, and the second one matters for intellectual honesty.

Finding A: the United States is the only market in this set where EV adoption went backwards in 2026. Europe up 35%, Canada forecast up 40%, Mexico up 44%, the U.S. down 28%. Four markets, four policy regimes, one outlier — and the outlier is the one that combined market exclusion with subsidy withdrawal in the same twelve months.

Finding B: cheap supply is necessary but not sufficient. Mexico has the second-cheapest entry EV in this comparison and the lowest BEV share — roughly 1.8%, well below the U.S. Availability of a $22,700 electric car has not by itself produced mass adoption there, because Mexican adoption is constrained by household income, charging infrastructure, and a strong consumer preference for conventional hybrids (69% of the electrified segment).

Finding B is a genuine limit on the argument and should be stated as one: removing the Chinese-EV exclusion would not, on its own, deliver European adoption rates to the U.S. What Finding B does not do is rescue the U.S. position — because the U.S. has high incomes and the densest fast-charging network of the four, i.e. exactly the preconditions Mexico lacks. America is the market where affordable supply would convert to adoption most efficiently, and it is the only one that has legislated that supply out of existence.

7The affordability test: normalizing for income and local price levels

The comparisons above are nominal. That is a real vulnerability, and it deserves to be tested rather than left for a reader to raise: a $29,000 car in a rich country may be a lighter burden than a $22,700 car in a poorer one. If the U.S. entry EV is proportionally affordable, the "price floor" argument weakens considerably.

What this does to the argument

It cuts the headline gap by roughly two-thirds. In nominal dollars the U.S. entry EV is about 60% above Canada's. Income-adjusted, the gap is roughly 13%, and the U.S. is essentially level with Germany. The claim that Americans face a uniquely severe EV affordability burden does not survive this adjustment, and should not be made.

A methodological caveat that cuts both ways: Normalization B compares PPP-adjusted incomes against nominal prices, which is not strictly consistent, and the underlying income series use different definitions and years. Treat it as directional, not precise.

What survives the adjustment — and it is most of the argument

  1. Canada still beats the United States on both normalizations — 40% vs 58%, and 0.38× vs 0.43×. Two neighbouring economies with comparable incomes, comparable regulation, and comparable consumer preferences, differing principally in their posture toward Chinese EVs. Normalization does not touch this comparison; it is the cleanest evidence in the section.
  1. Segment absence is not a ratio question. Normalization tells you whether the median household finds the entry EV proportionally affordable. It says nothing about the households whose new-car budget is $20,000–25,000 in nominal dollars — those households face an empty shelf regardless of what the U.S. median earns. A ratio describes the middle of a distribution; the policy removed the bottom of it.
  1. The domestic comparisons are entirely unaffected. The $6,500 gap between an Equinox EV and a gasoline Equinox, and the $7,500 the credit's expiry added to every affordable EV, involve no currency conversion, no income normalization, and no foreign market. They are the same for every American buyer.
  1. The §1.5 findings are unaffected. A 28% demand collapse alongside a 24% cut in EV incentives, a 2026-high transaction price, and a 2.5-point margin expansion are domestic facts about competitive pressure. No international normalization bears on them.

The corrected formulation

The right claim after this test is not "electric cars are unaffordable in America relative to peer markets." Proportionally, they are about as affordable as in Germany and considerably more so than in Mexico.

The right claim is narrower and holds firmly:

**The United States has a normal EV price level and an abnormal EV price floor.** Relative to local incomes and local vehicle prices, the American EV market is unremarkable in the middle and at the top. What distinguishes it is the complete absence of a bottom — no product below $28,995 — in the one market of the four with the incomes and the charging density to convert cheap supply into mass adoption most efficiently.

That is a claim about distribution, not level — and it is the claim the evidence in this section actually supports.

1.4The expiration of the federal EV tax credit

What ended, and when

The One Big Beautiful Bill Act, signed 4 July 2025, terminated the clean-vehicle credits for vehicles acquired after 30 September 2025:

  • §30D new clean vehicle credit — $7,500 — gone.
  • §25E used clean vehicle credit — $4,000 — gone.
  • §45W commercial clean vehicle credit — $7,500 — gone. This is the quiet one. The §45W "leasing loophole" had allowed leased EVs to capture the full $7,500 without the sourcing, assembly, and MSRP restrictions that applied to purchases. It was the main channel through which imported and premium EVs reached price-sensitive buyers. Its removal hit affordability harder than the headline §30D number suggests.

Nothing federal replaced them on a like-for-like basis.

The volume effect: a pull-forward, then a cliff

PeriodNew BEV salesEV share of new sales

The pattern is textbook incentive-cliff behaviour: demand was borrowed from the future into Q3 2025, then the market fell through the floor and has since found a lower equilibrium. Cox Automotive characterized Q1 2026 as a "necessary reset." Q2 2026 was the strongest quarter since the credit lapsed, but on a base roughly a fifth below the 2025 peak.

The counter-current is instructive: used EV sales rose 12% YoY in Q1 2026 to 93,500 units (+17% on Q4 2025), and average used EV listing prices fell 6.1% YoY to $34,653 in March 2026. Demand for electric vehicles did not disappear. It migrated to where the price was.

The price effect: two numbers that appear to contradict each other

This is where most commentary goes wrong, so the distinction is worth stating precisely.

  • Listing prices fell. Six months after the credit ended, the average new-EV listing price was $58,071, down 12.3% (Cars.com).
  • Transaction prices did not. The average new-EV transaction price in July 2026 was $56,126 — up 1.6% YoY and up 1.2% month-on-month (Kelley Blue Book).

Both are true, and reconciling them is the actual insight:

  1. Mix shift, not discounting. The buyers who vanished after September 2025 were disproportionately the marginal, price-sensitive ones. The models that kept selling skewed cheaper, and manufacturers pushed entry trims. A falling average listing price partly reflects a changed catalogue, not a cheaper car.
  2. Manufacturers absorbed the credit, then stopped. Through late 2025 and early 2026, automakers substituted their own incentives and aggressive lease subvention for the lost federal credit — effectively eating the $7,500 to hold volume. By mid-2026 they were pulling those discounts back, which is exactly what the rising transaction price shows.

So the honest answer to "did the credit's expiry raise EV prices?" is: it raised the effective out-of-pocket cost to the consumer by up to $7,500 immediately, while the sticker moved much less — and the sticker relief that did appear was a temporary, manufacturer-funded bridge that is now being withdrawn.

The origin restriction is the sharpest problem for affordability. The deduction requires U.S. final assembly — which disqualifies vehicles with VINs starting 2 (Canada), 3 (Mexico), J (Japan), W (Germany), or S (South Korea). Applied to America's cheapest EVs:

  • 2027 Chevrolet Bolt — Fairfax, Kansas → qualifies
  • 2026 Chevrolet Equinox EV — Ramos Arizpe, Mexico → does not qualify
  • 2026 Nissan Leaf — imported from Japan → does not qualify

Two of the three most affordable electric vehicles in the United States are excluded from the only federal benefit that replaced the credit. The replacement is therefore weakest precisely where affordability pressure is greatest.

Reach: from a national entitlement to a geographic lottery

The §30D credit's most underrated property was uniformity. A buyer in Mississippi and a buyer in Massachusetts got the same $7,500. Post-expiry, meaningful EV support is a state patchwork:

The federal credit's expiry did not just reduce the average subsidy — it redistributed EV affordability along state political lines, concentrating adoption further into markets that were already leading and stalling it in markets that were only just starting.

That is the sharpest "reach" consequence of the policy: EV adoption in the United States is becoming a function of your ZIP code's politics rather than your household's economics.

1.5Do U.S. manufacturers charge Americans more because of the ban?

The claim to be tested: protected from Chinese competition and shielded by tariffs, American manufacturers price their vehicles higher in the United States than they do in markets where they face real competition.

The intuition is sound and the mechanism is real. But the claim has to be stated carefully, because on a straight cross-border sticker comparison it does not hold — and an argument built on that comparison would collapse the first time anyone checked it. What follows tests the literal claim, shows why it fails, and then sets out the evidence that does support the underlying point. That evidence is stronger, not weaker.

Why the cross-border sticker test is the wrong test

Three structural reasons U.S. list prices sit below foreign ones, none of which have anything to do with competition:

  1. Home-market scale. The U.S. is the Mach-E's home volume market; Mexico and Germany are low-volume export markets where the car is a niche import carrying import logistics, homologation, small-network dealer margin, and a thin premium-buyer base.
  2. Tax and equipment. European prices embed VAT and a different standard-equipment and regulatory-compliance load.
  3. Positioning. An American brand in Europe is a specialty product priced against premium German marques, not against the volume segment it competes in at home.

A manufacturer's foreign price tells you about that foreign market's structure. It does not tell you what the manufacturer would charge Americans in a more competitive U.S. market. That counterfactual — not the cross-border spread — is the right benchmark, and it is where the evidence actually is.

The evidence that does support the claim: four findings

Finding 1 — Demand collapsed 28%, and discounts shrank anyway

This is the single most telling data point in the section.

In a competitively-disciplined market, a 28% year-on-year collapse in volume forces manufacturers to discount hard. That is what competition is. Instead, through 2026:

Finding 2 — Tariffs were passed through by shrinking the deal, not by raising the sticker

Industry reporting is explicit that automakers largely absorbed tariff costs into sticker prices and are instead letting the deals shrink. That single fact invalidates the sticker-comparison approach to this question. The price increase American consumers are paying is real — roughly $4,000–6,000 of the $8,000–12,000 rise in average new-vehicle prices since Q4 2024 is attributed to tariffs — but it is being delivered through the incentive line, where it is invisible on any MSRP table, foreign or domestic.

If you compare stickers, you will conclude nothing happened. That is why the comparison has to be made against discounts and margins instead.

Finding 4 — Segment abandonment: the affordable end was withdrawn, not just priced up

The clearest expression of reduced competitive pressure is that U.S. manufacturers stopped trying to serve the bottom of the market at all:

  • Combined EV write-downs across Ford, GM, and Stellantis now exceed $53 billion.
  • Ford killed the F-150 Lightning in December 2025 in a $19.5bn write-down and scrapped its next-generation electric truck programme (T3).
  • Ford's Tennessee EV plant was converted to gasoline truck production — 1,000 hires to add 50,000 conventional F-150 and Super Duty units in 2026.
  • GM paused next-generation electric pickups and appears to have shelved the next-generation affordable EV once planned for Fairfax.

Firms facing a $20,000 competitor cannot abandon the $20,000 segment. Firms that have had that competitor legislated away can — and, on this evidence, do.

The historical precedent: this has been measured before

The U.S. has run this experiment. In May 1981 Japan agreed to a Voluntary Export Restraint limiting car exports to the United States, explicitly to give Detroit room to retool. The measured consequences:

  • Between April 1981 and April 1984, suggested retail prices of Japanese models in the U.S. rose 15.8% — about 5.3% a year — with nearly the entire increase explained by upgrading rather than inflation.
  • Robert Feenstra's work established that roughly two-thirds of the price increase came from quality and trim upgrading: constrained on units, manufacturers shifted the mix toward higher-specification, higher-margin cars.
  • Over 1986–1990, the restraint left Japanese cars in the U.S. about $1,200 more expensive (1983 dollars), roughly 14% above the unrestrained counterfactual.
  • The distributional effect was specific: "This upgrading may benefit consumers who would purchase a luxury import in any case, but harms those who desire the basic imports."

That last sentence, written about 1981, describes the 2026 EV market exactly. Quantity restriction does not usually show up as a visible price increase on a like-for-like car. It shows up as the cheap version quietly ceasing to exist, and the average price rising because the mix moved upmarket. Which is precisely the pattern in §1.3, Comparison 2 — a U.S. EV ladder with no rungs below $29,000.

The claim, correctly stated

The evidence does not support "American manufacturers charge more in America than abroad." On stickers they charge notably less here than in Mexico or Germany, and that is explained by market structure rather than by generosity.

What the evidence does support is stronger and more directly harmful to the U.S. consumer:

Shielded from low-cost competition by an ownership-based import prohibition and a 25% tariff wall, U.S. manufacturers are charging American consumers more than a competitive American market would permit — extracting it through withdrawn discounts, upmarket mix, and the abandonment of the affordable segment rather than through higher list prices. Demand fell 28%, incentives fell 24%, transaction prices hit a 2026 high, and North American margins expanded 2.5 points in the same twelve months.

The right comparison is not America against Mexico. It is America against the America that would exist if a $22,000 electric car were legal to sell here — and the 1981 VER gives a measured precedent for what that difference costs: an upmarket mix shift that leaves buyers of premium vehicles roughly indifferent and buyers of basic vehicles with nothing to buy.

1.6The two policies interact — and the interaction is the point

Taken separately, each policy has a defensible industrial logic. Taken together, they produce a specific and probably unintended consumer outcome:

  Chinese-EV exclusion  ──►  removes the $18k–23k segment  ──►  raises the FLOOR
                                                                      │
                                                                      ▼
                                                          Entry EV ≈ $29,000

Before September 2025, an American could buy an Equinox EV at roughly $28,000 net of the credit. Today the same car is about $36,500, with no federal credit, and the loan-interest deduction does not apply to it because it is built in Mexico. That is an effective ~$8,500 increase in the cost of the most popular affordable EV in America, delivered entirely by policy in under a year.

Simultaneously, the vehicle that would ordinarily arrive to fill that vacated price point — a $20,000-class Chinese city EV, on sale in Mexico City, and shortly in Toronto, and already in Berlin — is not merely expensive in the U.S. It is illegal to sell.

The domestic industry got the protection. The American consumer paid for it in both directions at once.

And per §1.5, the protection is being converted into margin rather than into cheaper cars: through 2026 EV demand fell 28% while EV incentives fell 24%, transaction prices reached a 2026 high, and GM's North American margin expanded 2.5 points. The savings from excluding a low-cost competitor did not reach the showroom floor.
