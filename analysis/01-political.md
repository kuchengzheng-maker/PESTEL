# 1. Political

**Brief:** How have (a) the ban on Chinese EVs and (b) the expiration of the federal
EV tax credit affected the **pricing** and **reach** of electric vehicles for American
consumers? For the Chinese-EV ban, benchmark U.S. prices against Mexico, Canada,
and Europe.

**Baseline:** September 2026

---

## 1.0 The core finding

Two political decisions — one that removed the cheapest possible supply, and one that
removed the largest demand-side subsidy — have moved in the same direction at the same
time. Together they have done something unusual: they raised the **floor** price of an
electric car in America while simultaneously lowering the **ceiling** on how many
Americans can afford one.

The result is a market that has stopped being a mass market and reverted to a
discretionary one. U.S. EV share peaked at **7.5% of new-vehicle sales in Q3 2025**,
collapsed to **5.8% in Q4 2025**, and was still at roughly **5.8–6%** through the first
half of 2026. Meanwhile the cheapest new EV an American can buy costs about **$29,000**,
against roughly **$18,000 in Canada**, **$22,700 in Mexico**, and **$23,200 in Europe**.

Two control comparisons pin the cause down. First, an identical non-Chinese vehicle —
the Tesla Model Y Standard — costs within about 10% of the same amount in all four
markets on a tax-adjusted basis, which rules out currency, cost, tax, and regulatory
explanations for the entry-level gap. Second, the United States is the **only** one of
these four markets where EV adoption went *backwards* in 2026: Europe +35%, Canada
forecast +40%, Mexico +44%, the U.S. **−28%**.

Neither policy is an accident or a side-effect. Both are the intended operation of
deliberate industrial policy. The consumer-facing cost of that policy is the subject of
this section.

---

## 1.1 The "ban" on Chinese EVs is not one policy — it is three walls

It is analytically important to separate the instruments, because they behave
differently and they fail differently. Only one of them is an actual ban.

### Wall 1 — Section 301 tariff: 100% on Chinese EVs

The Section 301 tariff on Chinese-built battery-electric vehicles was raised from 25%
to **100%**, effective 27 September 2024, and has been maintained through 2026. Stacked
with the 2.5% MFN passenger-car duty, a Chinese-built EV faces roughly **102.5%** before
any other measure.

This is a *price* instrument. It does not prohibit the vehicle; it prices it out.

### Wall 2 — Section 232 automotive tariff: 25% on imported vehicles

Separately, the Section 232 national-security tariff imposed **25% on imported passenger
vehicles from 3 April 2025** and on parts from 3 May 2025, with partial carve-outs for
USMCA-compliant goods. This is not China-specific — it hits German, Japanese, Korean,
and UK imports too — but it stacks on top of Walls 1 and 3 and it is a major reason
*every* EV in America got more expensive, not just Chinese ones.

Its scale is significant: average new-vehicle prices in Q1 2026 ran **$8,000–12,000
above Q4 2024 levels**, of which roughly **$4,000–6,000 is attributed to the tariff
component**. Imported models absorbed **$5,000–8,900** per vehicle; even
domestically-assembled models rose **$1,600–2,000** on steel, aluminum, and parts costs.

### Wall 3 — The connected-vehicle rule: the actual ban

This is the decisive instrument, and it is the one most commentary under-weights.

On 16 January 2025 the Commerce Department's Bureau of Industry and Security finalized
*Securing the ICTS Supply Chain: Connected Vehicles*, effective 17 March 2025. It
prohibits the import or sale of connected vehicles containing covered software or
vehicle-connectivity hardware with a Chinese or Russian nexus:

- **Software prohibitions bite from model year 2027** — i.e. vehicles on sale now.
- **Hardware prohibitions bite from model year 2030** (or 1 January 2029 for hardware
  not tied to a model year).
- Critically, a manufacturer **owned by, controlled by, or subject to the jurisdiction
  of China** is barred from selling a completed connected vehicle in the U.S. from MY2027
  **regardless of where the software or hardware came from, and regardless of where the
  car was assembled**.

That last clause is the whole ballgame. It converts a tariff problem into a market-access
prohibition. BYD cannot tariff-engineer its way in by assembling in Mexico, Hungary, or
Brazil, because the prohibition attaches to the *manufacturer's ownership*, not the
vehicle's origin. There is no price at which the vehicle becomes legal.

A pending bill, the **Connected Vehicle Security Act of 2026** (Sens. Moreno and
Slotkin), would codify and broaden this into a statutory ban on Chinese-linked vehicles
and components — hardening what is currently a regulation into law, and insulating it
from a future administration's rulemaking.

### Why the distinction matters for consumers

| Instrument | Type | Can it be engineered around? | Consumer effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| Section 301 (100%) | Price | Yes — assemble outside China | Raises price of Chinese-built EVs to non-viability |
| Section 232 (25%) | Price | Partly — USMCA compliance, U.S. assembly | Raises price of **all** imported EVs, ~$5,000–8,900 |
| BIS connected-vehicle rule | Prohibition | **No** — attaches to ownership | Removes the entire sub-$25,000 competitive set from the market |

If only Walls 1 and 2 existed, the U.S. would have expensive Chinese EVs. With Wall 3,
it has **none**, at any price, for the foreseeable future. The policy question is
therefore not "how much do tariffs add to the sticker?" It is "what does it cost the
American consumer to have an entire price segment removed from the market?"

## 1.2 The comparative price benchmark: Mexico, Canada, Europe

Three peer markets have made three genuinely different political choices about Chinese
EVs. That makes them an unusually clean natural experiment.

### The policy regimes

| Market | Policy toward Chinese EVs (Sept 2026) | Character |
|---|---|---|
| **United States** | 100% §301 + 25% §232 + BIS ownership-based prohibition from MY2027 | **Exclusion** |
| **Mexico** | Tariff raised 20% → **50%** on Chinese-built cars, effective 1 Jan 2026. No security ban. Chinese brands sell freely. | **Taxed but open** |
| **Canada** | 100% surtax **repealed effective 1 March 2026**, replaced by **6.1% MFN duty inside an annual quota** (24,500 units in the first half-year; ~49,000/yr, rising toward 70,000 by 2030) under a Carney-era strategic partnership with China. | **Managed re-opening** |
| **European Union** | Countervailing duties from Oct 2024 on top of the 10% base duty — **BYD 17.0%, Geely 18.8%, SAIC 35.3%, other cooperating ~20.7%**; from Jan 2026 a **minimum-import-price undertaking mechanism** offers an alternative to duties. PHEV duties in preparation as of June 2026. | **Calibrated / negotiated** |

Note the direction of travel: **the U.S. is the only one of the four that has moved from
a price instrument to a prohibition.** Canada has moved in the opposite direction
entirely — it repealed the very measure it had adopted in 2024 to mirror Washington.

### The entry-level price comparison

Prices are for each market's cheapest mainstream new battery-electric vehicle. USD
conversions are indicative, at approximately USD/MXN 17.6, USD/EUR 0.86, USD/CAD 1.385.

| Market | Cheapest new BEV | Local price | ≈ USD | Tax basis |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **United States** | 2027 Chevrolet Bolt LT | $27,600 + $1,395 dest. = **$28,995** | **$28,995** | Ex sales tax |
| | 2026 Nissan Leaf S+ | $29,990 + $1,495 dest. = **$31,485** | **$31,485** | Ex sales tax |
| | 2026 Chevrolet Equinox EV LT1 | ~**$36,495** w/ dest. | **$36,495** | Ex sales tax |
| **Mexico** | BYD Dolphin Mini (300 km) | **MXN 399,800** | **≈ $22,700** | Incl. IVA |
| | BYD Dolphin Mini (380 km) | MXN 415,800 | ≈ $23,600 | Incl. IVA |
| **Canada** | BYD entry model (Seagull), launch confirmed for late 2026 | **C$25,000** | **≈ $18,050** | Ex GST/PST |
| **Europe** | BYD Dolphin Surf Active (DE) | **€22,990** (launch promo €19,990) | **≈ $26,700** (promo ≈ $23,200) | **Incl. 19% VAT** |
| | BYD Dolphin Surf (IT, pre-incentive) | €18,990 | ≈ $22,100 | **Incl. 22% VAT** |
| | Same, German price **ex-VAT** | €19,320 | ≈ $22,500 | Ex VAT — like-for-like with U.S. |

### Reading the table honestly — three caveats

**Caveat 1 — tax basis.** European prices are quoted VAT-inclusive; U.S. prices are
quoted before sales tax. Stripping German VAT puts the Dolphin Surf at roughly
**$22,500** on the same basis as a U.S. sticker. That *widens* the real gap rather
than narrowing it.

**Caveat 2 — segment.** The Dolphin Mini / Dolphin Surf / Seagull is an A-segment city
car (~3.8 m, 30 kWh, 220–300 km). The Bolt is a B-segment hatch with 262 miles EPA range
and a 65 kWh pack. These are not the same product, and a straight price line
under-states the Bolt's content.

But that caveat is itself the finding. **The United States has no A-segment EV at all.**
Not an expensive one — none. The comparison isn't "American A-segment cars cost more";
it's that the segment does not exist here, because policy removed the only firms
currently willing to build in it at volume. Mexico, Canada, and Europe all have a
~$18,000–23,000 electric car on sale. America's price floor starts around $29,000, and
that floor is set by regulation, not by engineering.

**Caveat 3 — Canada's number is a launch commitment, not yet a transacted price.**
BYD confirmed Canadian entry on 8 June 2026 — 20+ dealerships in Toronto, Vancouver,
Montreal, and Calgary, four models (Seagull, Dolphin, Atto 3, Seal), **from C$25,000 to
C$44,990**. Only the C$25,000 entry price is company-confirmed; the rest are dealer
guidance and overseas comparables. Treat the Canadian figure as a forward indicator.

### What the three regimes actually demonstrate

- **Mexico proves tariffs are a price instrument, not a wall.** A 50% duty cut Chinese
  car imports 45.3% year-on-year in January 2026 (to US$188m) — a real effect. But BYD
  still lists the Dolphin Mini at MXN 399,800 and still sells it. Mexican consumers pay
  more than they would in a free market; they are not denied the product. The Mexican
  government forecasts **MXN 52bn (~US$2.8bn)** in 2026 revenue from the measure.
- **Canada proves the exclusion was a political choice, not a technical necessity.**
  Ottawa adopted a 100% surtax in 2024 explicitly to align with Washington, collected
  roughly **$2m in total revenue** from it (because it blocked everything), and repealed
  it in March 2026 for a 6.1% in-quota duty forecast to raise **over $100m a year**.
  Canada concluded that a calibrated duty beat a prohibition on both revenue and consumer
  grounds. The same continent, the same security literature, the opposite answer.
- **Europe proves a middle path is administrable.** Firm-specific countervailing duties
  (17.0%–35.3%) scaled to each manufacturer's subsidy level, now supplemented by a
  minimum-price undertaking mechanism, have protected European producers while leaving a
  €19,990 electric car on the market. BYD became **Germany's best-selling PHEV brand in
  May 2026** — evidence that the EU's approach constrains rather than excludes.

### The bottom line on the ban

For the American consumer, the ban's price effect is not a markup that can be measured
on any window sticker. It is an **absence**. The measurable consequence is that the U.S.
entry price for an electric car sits roughly **$6,000–11,000 above** that of every
comparable market — and unlike a tariff, that gap cannot be competed away, because the
competitor is legally barred rather than merely taxed.

The reach effect follows arithmetically. An $18,000–23,000 EV is a plausible second
household car, a first car for a young buyer, and a viable purchase for the ~40% of U.S.
households whose new-vehicle budget tops out near $30,000. A $29,000-and-up EV market is
a market for people who were probably buying a new car anyway.

## 1.3 Six further comparisons

The A-segment comparison in §1.2 is the headline, but it rests on a single non-identical
vehicle class. The following six comparisons attack the same question from independent
angles — three cross-border, three internal to the U.S. market. They converge.

---

### Comparison 1 — The same-model control: Tesla Model Y Standard in four markets

If American EVs were expensive because of general cost, currency, labour, or
market-structure differences, then *every* EV would be more expensive here, not just the
cheap ones. The cleanest way to test that is to price an identical, non-Chinese vehicle
across all four markets.

The Model Y Standard is the ideal control: same manufacturer, near-identical
specification, sold in all four markets, and completely unaffected by Chinese-EV policy.

| Market | Local price | Consumption tax in sticker | Price **ex-tax** | ≈ USD ex-tax |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **United States** | $39,990 | none | $39,990 | **$39,990** |
| **Canada** | C$49,990 | none (GST/PST added at sale) | C$49,990 | **≈ $36,100** |
| **Germany** | €39,990 | 19% VAT included | €33,605 | **≈ $39,100** |
| **Mexico** | MXN 799,000 | 16% IVA included | MXN 688,800 | **≈ $39,100** |

**The spread is about 10%, and the U.S. is not the outlier.** On a like-for-like,
tax-adjusted basis a Model Y costs essentially the same in Austin, Berlin, and Mexico
City, and slightly *less* in Toronto.

This is the single most important comparison in the section, because of what it rules
out. The U.S. entry-level premium documented in §1.2 is **not** an artifact of exchange
rates, tax accounting, labour costs, dealer margins, safety regulation, or American
consumer preference for larger vehicles. Those forces would show up in the Model Y row,
and they don't.

The gap exists at the bottom of the market and only at the bottom of the market — which
is precisely where the excluded competitor would have sat.

---

### Comparison 2 — The price ladder: how many rungs exist below $25,000

A market's affordability is better described by its ladder than by its average.

| Market | Rungs below ≈$25,000 (USD equiv.) | Entry rung | Ladder shape |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Mexico** | Several — BYD Dolphin Mini 300 km and 380 km, plus rival Chinese entries | ≈$22,700 | Continuous from ≈$22,700 to ≈$79,500 (BYD Tang EV, MXN 1,399,000) |
| **Canada** (from late 2026) | Multiple — BYD Seagull and Dolphin confirmed | ≈$18,050 | C$25,000–C$44,990 confirmed across four models |
| **Europe** | Several — BYD Dolphin Surf, plus Dacia Spring, Citroën ë-C3, Leapmotor T03 and other A-segment entries | ≈$22,500 ex-VAT | Continuous from ≈€19,000 to €44,990 (BYD Seal) and up |
| **United States** | **Zero** | **$28,995** | Ladder simply begins ~$29,000 |

The U.S. does not have an expensive bottom rung. **It has no bottom rung.** Every other
market in this comparison offers a continuous price ladder starting in the high teens or
low twenties; the American ladder starts at $29,000 and the space beneath it is empty.

That emptiness is the mechanism by which the policy reaches consumers. A household with
a $25,000 ceiling is not offered a worse EV in America — it is offered none.

---

### Comparison 3 — The outcome comparison: BEV share of new-vehicle sales

Price is an input. Adoption is the output. This comparison is where the "reach" question
is actually answered — and it is also where the analysis has to be careful, because the
result is not uniformly flattering to the simple story.

| Market | BEV share of new sales | Direction in 2026 |
|---|---|---|
| **Europe** | **22%** (H1 2026); 24% in June. Germany 26%, France 28%, Norway 98%, Denmark 80% | **↑ BEV registrations +35% YoY** |
| **Canada** | **≈10.5%** BEV (13.1% forecast for BEV+PHEV combined) | **↑ EV sales forecast +40.2% for 2026** |
| **United States** | **5.8%** (Q1 2026), ≈6% (Q2) | **↓ new BEV sales −28% YoY** |
| **Mexico** | **≈1.8%** BEV (electrified vehicles are 12.4% of light-vehicle sales; BEVs are 14.49% of that segment) | **↑ electrified sales +44% YoY; BYD holds ~70% of EV+PHEV sales** |

Two findings, and the second one matters for intellectual honesty.

**Finding A: the United States is the only market in this set where EV adoption went
backwards in 2026.** Europe up 35%, Canada forecast up 40%, Mexico up 44%, the U.S. down
28%. Four markets, four policy regimes, one outlier — and the outlier is the one that
combined market exclusion with subsidy withdrawal in the same twelve months.

**Finding B: cheap supply is necessary but not sufficient.** Mexico has the second-cheapest
entry EV in this comparison and the *lowest* BEV share — roughly 1.8%, well below the U.S.
Availability of a $22,700 electric car has not by itself produced mass adoption there,
because Mexican adoption is constrained by household income, charging infrastructure, and
a strong consumer preference for conventional hybrids (69% of the electrified segment).

Finding B is a genuine limit on the argument and should be stated as one: removing the
Chinese-EV exclusion would not, on its own, deliver European adoption rates to the U.S.
What Finding B does *not* do is rescue the U.S. position — because the U.S. has high
incomes and the densest fast-charging network of the four, i.e. exactly the preconditions
Mexico lacks. America is the market where affordable supply would convert to adoption most
efficiently, and it is the only one that has legislated that supply out of existence.

### Comparison 4 — Before and after the credit, holding the sticker constant

This isolates the tax-credit variable cleanly: same car, same MSRP, different policy year.
The "with credit" column is what a buyer paid before 30 September 2025; the current column
is what the same vehicle costs today.

| Model (2026, incl. destination) | Price today | Net with $7,500 credit | Effective increase | Qualifies for the replacement loan-interest deduction? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2027 Chevrolet Bolt LT | $28,995 | $21,495 | **+$7,500** | **Yes** — Fairfax, Kansas |
| 2026 Nissan Leaf S+ | $31,485 | $23,985 | **+$7,500** | **No** — imported from Japan |
| 2026 Chevrolet Equinox EV LT1 | $36,495 | $28,995 | **+$7,500** | **No** — Ramos Arizpe, Mexico |
| 2026 Tesla Model Y Standard | $39,990 | $32,490 | **+$7,500** | **Yes** — U.S.-assembled |
| 2026 Hyundai Ioniq 5 SEL RWD | $41,400 | $33,900 | **+$7,500** | **Yes** — Georgia |

Three observations:

1. **Every affordable EV in America got $7,500 more expensive to the buyer in one day**,
   with no corresponding change in the product.
2. The §45W lease channel meant the "with credit" column was reachable **regardless of
   assembly location or MSRP caps** — a leased imported EV captured the full $7,500. Its
   repeal removed the credit *and* the workaround simultaneously.
3. The replacement benefit is inversely correlated with need. The two cheapest EVs on this
   list are the two that don't qualify for it.

### Comparison 5 — The EV against its own gasoline twin

The most consumer-legible comparison is not international at all. It is the EV sitting
next to the identical gasoline model in the same showroom.

| Gasoline model | Price | Electric counterpart | Price | Gap today | Gap with the $7,500 credit |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2026 Chevrolet Equinox | $29,995 | Chevrolet Equinox EV LT1 | $36,495 | **+$6,500** | **−$1,000** (the EV was *cheaper*) |
| 2026 Chevrolet Trax | $22,995 | 2027 Chevrolet Bolt LT | $28,995 | **+$6,000** | **−$1,500** (illustrative) |

This is the clearest statement of what the credit's expiry did. Under the credit, the
electric version of a mainstream American crossover **undercut its own gasoline sibling
at the point of sale**. That is the threshold at which a technology stops requiring
advocacy and starts selling on arithmetic.

Today the same electric model carries a **$6,000–6,500 premium** over the gasoline car
parked beside it, and the buyer must recover that through fuel savings over years of
ownership. The Bolt row is illustrative rather than historical — the 2027 Bolt arrived
after the credit lapsed and never benefited from it — which is itself the point: the car
GM designed to be America's affordable EV launched into a market where the policy that
would have made it cheaper than a Trax no longer exists.

### Comparison 6 — EVs against the broader new-vehicle market

Finally, the EV segment against the market it is trying to join.

| Metric (2026) | Value |
|---|---|
| Average new **EV** transaction price (July 2026) | **$56,126** — up 1.6% YoY |
| Average new **vehicle** price, all powertrains | **≈$48,841**; record ATP ≈$50,364 in early 2026 |
| **EV premium over the market average** | **≈$6,000–7,300** |
| Average **used EV** listing price (March 2026) | **$34,653** — down 6.1% YoY |

The EV segment sits roughly $6,000–7,300 above the average new vehicle, and the gap is
widening again as manufacturers withdraw the incentives they used to substitute for the
federal credit through early 2026.

Note where demand went instead: used EV sales rose 12% year-on-year in Q1 2026 to 93,500
units while used prices fell 6.1% to $34,653. American demand for electric vehicles did
not evaporate — **it relocated to the only part of the market where the price was allowed
to fall.** The used market is functioning as the affordable EV segment that policy
prevents the new market from having.

### What the six comparisons establish together

| Comparison | What it rules out or establishes |
|---|---|
| 1. Model Y control | Rules out cost, FX, tax, and regulatory explanations for the entry-price gap |
| 2. Price ladder | Establishes the gap is a missing *segment*, not a uniform markup |
| 3. BEV share | Establishes the U.S. is the only market where adoption reversed — while conceding cheap supply alone is insufficient (Mexico) |
| 4. Before/after credit | Isolates a clean +$7,500 consumer cost increase, unrelated to the product |
| 5. EV vs. gas twin | Shows the credit had achieved sticker parity, and its removal reopened a $6,000+ gap |
| 6. EV vs. market | Shows the segment sits $6,000–7,300 above market average, with demand displaced into used vehicles |

No single comparison carries the argument. Together they make it difficult to attribute
the American EV affordability gap to anything other than the two political decisions
examined in this section.

## 1.4 The expiration of the federal EV tax credit

### What ended, and when

The **One Big Beautiful Bill Act**, signed 4 July 2025, terminated the clean-vehicle
credits for vehicles acquired after **30 September 2025**:

- **§30D new clean vehicle credit — $7,500** — gone.
- **§25E used clean vehicle credit — $4,000** — gone.
- **§45W commercial clean vehicle credit — $7,500** — gone. This is the quiet one. The
  §45W "leasing loophole" had allowed leased EVs to capture the full $7,500 *without*
  the sourcing, assembly, and MSRP restrictions that applied to purchases. It was the
  main channel through which imported and premium EVs reached price-sensitive buyers.
  Its removal hit affordability harder than the headline §30D number suggests.

Nothing federal replaced them on a like-for-like basis.

### The volume effect: a pull-forward, then a cliff

| Period | New BEV sales | EV share of new sales |
|---|---|---|
| Q3 2025 (pre-deadline surge) | — | **7.5% — all-time peak** |
| Q4 2025 (post-deadline) | — | **5.8%** |
| Q1 2026 | **212,600** (−28% YoY from 296,304) | **5.8%** |
| Q2 2026 | **247,226** (+14.2% QoQ) | **≈6%, stabilizing** |

The pattern is textbook incentive-cliff behaviour: demand was borrowed from the future
into Q3 2025, then the market fell through the floor and has since found a lower
equilibrium. Cox Automotive characterized Q1 2026 as a "necessary reset." Q2 2026 was
the strongest quarter since the credit lapsed, but on a base roughly a fifth below the
2025 peak.

The counter-current is instructive: **used EV sales rose 12% YoY in Q1 2026 to 93,500
units** (+17% on Q4 2025), and average used EV listing prices fell 6.1% YoY to **$34,653**
in March 2026. Demand for electric vehicles did not disappear. It migrated to where the
price was.

### The price effect: two numbers that appear to contradict each other

This is where most commentary goes wrong, so the distinction is worth stating precisely.

- **Listing prices fell.** Six months after the credit ended, the average new-EV *listing*
  price was **$58,071, down 12.3%** (Cars.com).
- **Transaction prices did not.** The average new-EV *transaction* price in July 2026 was
  **$56,126 — up 1.6% YoY and up 1.2% month-on-month** (Kelley Blue Book).

Both are true, and reconciling them is the actual insight:

1. **Mix shift, not discounting.** The buyers who vanished after September 2025 were
   disproportionately the marginal, price-sensitive ones. The models that kept selling
   skewed cheaper, and manufacturers pushed entry trims. A falling average listing price
   partly reflects a changed catalogue, not a cheaper car.
2. **Manufacturers absorbed the credit, then stopped.** Through late 2025 and early 2026,
   automakers substituted their own incentives and aggressive lease subvention for the
   lost federal credit — effectively eating the $7,500 to hold volume. By mid-2026 they
   were **pulling those discounts back**, which is exactly what the rising transaction
   price shows.

So the honest answer to "did the credit's expiry raise EV prices?" is: **it raised the
effective out-of-pocket cost to the consumer by up to $7,500 immediately, while the
sticker moved much less** — and the sticker relief that did appear was a temporary,
manufacturer-funded bridge that is now being withdrawn.

### What replaced it — and why it is much weaker

OBBBA introduced an **auto loan interest deduction: up to $10,000 of annual interest
on a new vehicle loan, 2025–2028**. It is available for EVs on the same terms as any
other vehicle. But it is a far smaller and far narrower benefit:

| | §30D credit (to Sept 2025) | Loan interest deduction (2025–2028) |
|---|---|---|
| Form | **Tax credit** — dollar-for-dollar | **Deduction** — worth only your marginal rate |
| Timing | **Point of sale** — cut the price you financed | Claimed at filing, spread over years |
| Typical value | **$7,500** | Roughly **$300–1,000/yr** in actual tax saved |
| Cash buyers | Eligible | **Ineligible** — you must finance |
| Vehicle origin | U.S./North American assembly rules | **U.S. final assembly only** (VIN 1, 4, 5, 7) |
| Income limits | $150k/$300k MAGI | Phases out from $100k single / $200k joint, gone by $150k/$250k |

The origin restriction is the sharpest problem for affordability. The deduction requires
U.S. final assembly — which disqualifies vehicles with VINs starting 2 (Canada), 3
(Mexico), J (Japan), W (Germany), or S (South Korea). Applied to America's cheapest EVs:

- **2027 Chevrolet Bolt** — Fairfax, Kansas → **qualifies**
- **2026 Chevrolet Equinox EV** — Ramos Arizpe, Mexico → **does not qualify**
- **2026 Nissan Leaf** — imported from Japan → **does not qualify**

Two of the three most affordable electric vehicles in the United States are excluded
from the only federal benefit that replaced the credit. The replacement is therefore
weakest precisely where affordability pressure is greatest.

### Reach: from a national entitlement to a geographic lottery

The §30D credit's most underrated property was **uniformity**. A buyer in Mississippi and
a buyer in Massachusetts got the same $7,500. Post-expiry, meaningful EV support is a
state patchwork:

| State | Program | Value |
|---|---|---|
| California | MyFirstEV (launching summer 2026) | **$3,500** new (<$50k MSRP); **$1,750** used (<$25k); no income cap, first-time ZEV buyers |
| California | Clean Cars 4 All (income-qualified) | up to **~$12,000** stacked, varies by air district |
| Colorado | Innovative Motor Vehicle Credit (refundable) | **$750** base + **$2,500** if MSRP <$35,000 |
| New York | Drive Clean Rebate | up to **$2,000** |
| Texas, Florida, most of the South and Mountain West | — | **$0** |

A Colorado buyer of a sub-$35,000 EV can still assemble **$3,250** in state support; a
Texas buyer of the identical car gets nothing. The federal credit's expiry did not just
reduce the average subsidy — it **redistributed EV affordability along state political
lines**, concentrating adoption further into markets that were already leading and
stalling it in markets that were only just starting.

That is the sharpest "reach" consequence of the policy: EV adoption in the United States
is becoming a function of your ZIP code's politics rather than your household's economics.

## 1.5 The two policies interact — and the interaction is the point

Taken separately, each policy has a defensible industrial logic. Taken together, they
produce a specific and probably unintended consumer outcome:

```
  Chinese-EV exclusion  ──►  removes the $18k–23k segment  ──►  raises the FLOOR
                                                                      │
                                                                      ▼
                                                          Entry EV ≈ $29,000
                                                                      ▲
                                                                      │
  Tax-credit expiry     ──►  removes $7,500 at point of sale  ──►  lowers the CEILING
                             (and the §45W lease channel)          of who can reach it
```

Before September 2025, an American could buy an Equinox EV at roughly **$28,000 net**
of the credit. Today the same car is about **$36,500**, with no federal credit, and the
loan-interest deduction does not apply to it because it is built in Mexico. That is an
**effective ~$8,500 increase in the cost of the most popular affordable EV in America**,
delivered entirely by policy in under a year.

Simultaneously, the vehicle that would ordinarily arrive to fill that vacated price
point — a $20,000-class Chinese city EV, on sale in Mexico City, and shortly in Toronto,
and already in Berlin — is not merely expensive in the U.S. It is illegal to sell.

**The domestic industry got the protection. The American consumer paid for it in both
directions at once.**

## 1.6 Political outlook and watch items (2026–2030)

| Watch item | Timing | Why it matters to price and reach |
|---|---|---|
| **Connected Vehicle Security Act of 2026** | Pending | Would harden the regulatory ban into statute, making reversal a legislative rather than executive act — effectively locking the price floor in for a decade |
| **MY2030 hardware prohibitions** | 2029–2030 | Extends the ban from software to Chinese-origin connectivity hardware; may pull in non-Chinese OEMs' supply chains and add cost across the board |
| **Canada's quota performance** | Mar 2026 – Feb 2027 | The first real-world data on Chinese EV pricing in a North American market. Direct, visible cross-border price comparison for U.S. consumers and a live rebuttal to the "no other option" framing |
| **Section 232 review / trade deals** | Ongoing | The 25% import tariff is the swing factor for non-Chinese EV prices; relief here would cut $5,000–8,900 from imported models faster than any subsidy could |
| **State-level backfill** | 2026–2028 | Whether more states adopt California/Colorado-style rebates determines how wide the geographic adoption gap becomes |
| **2028 sunset of the loan-interest deduction** | Dec 2028 | Removes the last federal consumer-side EV benefit unless renewed |
| **EU minimum-price mechanism outcomes** | 2026–2027 | If it works, it becomes the template for a "third way" the U.S. has so far declined to consider |
